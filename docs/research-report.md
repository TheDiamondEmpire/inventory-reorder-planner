# Inventory & Reorder Planner: Research Report

> **Where the code is.** The experiment scripts that produced these results are part of the source package, which is distributed separately (see [SOURCE-ACCESS.md](../SOURCE-ACCESS.md)). The raw results they wrote are included in this repository under [`results/`](../results/).

> **Status: protocol v0.2, results recorded (2026-11-24).** Questions, methods and the statistical plan were written down before the full experiments ran (v0.1); v0.2 adds the results and logs every deviation in the [change log](#11-change-log). **Every item, supplier, demand series and workload in this report is synthetic (generated by `irp.generator` and the experiment scripts); nothing was measured on a real shop, real stock or real customers.** The numbers in section 7 are copied from `results/summary.md`, which `experiments/summarize.py` generates from the raw per-run files in `results/`.
>
> **The product turns recorded sales and supplier terms into estimates.** Correctness below means "the calculators agree with independent references under the assumptions they state", and "the simulator's policies behave as measured", not "the suggested quantity is right for any particular business".

## Abstract

This study asks four things about a small local stock and reorder planner built on the Python standard library: (E1) which of its forecasting methods is most accurate on which demand pattern; (E2) how reorder policies compare in a simulator that runs the planner's own code, and whether the fill rate they achieve matches the service target they were sized for; (E3) whether its calculators agree with independent references and whether the checks can catch deliberate bugs; (E4) what request rate its server sustains on the busy machine it was built on. Results on synthetic data only. (E1) With 60 paired seeds per pattern and rolling-origin evaluation, simple exponential smoothing had the lowest mean absolute error on smooth, seasonal and trending weekly demand at a one-week horizon; seasonal-naive won only on the seasonal pattern at a four-week horizon and was the worst method by far on a trend; Croston with the Syntetos-Boylan correction won on intermittent and lumpy demand, by margins of about 1%. (E2) In 30 paired seeds per cell, (s,Q) and (s,S) were indistinguishable in 14 of 15 pattern and target cells; weekly periodic review held about half the inventory but placed about five times as many orders and cost far more at the assumed order cost; sizing for a cycle service level of 95% delivered a fill rate of 99.5% on smooth demand, and the delivered fill rate was above the named percentage in 31 of 45 cells, consistent in 8 and below in 6; the delivered cycle service level fell short of its target for seasonal, trending and lumpy demand. (E3) The product passed all 17 checks against a Monte Carlo reference, exact Poisson enumeration, brute-force search and independent re-implementations, and all 17 deliberately broken variants were caught; the normal approximation under a random lead time was off by up to 0.0056 in service level, and it is conservative for slow items. (E4) With one server process and a Python load generator on the same machine, whose system CPU load was 68% to 100% before every run, median throughput was 11.0 requests/s with one client thread, 13.2 with four and 16.9 with eight (the 95% intervals overlap), with no errors in 1,587 requests and median p95 latencies of 592, 1,237 and 2,106 ms. None of this predicts behaviour on real demand, real suppliers or real people.

## 1. Research questions

- **E1.** Which forecasting method (naive benchmark, moving average, simple exponential smoothing, seasonal-naive, Croston, Croston with the Syntetos-Boylan correction) has the lowest error on weekly demand of five generated patterns (smooth, seasonal, trending, intermittent, lumpy), evaluated by rolling origin at horizons of one and four weeks, and for which patterns is the answer clear and for which is it within noise?
- **E2.** How do (s,Q), (s,S) and periodic-review (R=7 days, order-up-to) policies compare on fill rate, stockout events, average inventory and cost in a discrete-event simulator that calls the planner's own estimation and policy code, at service targets of 90%, 95% and 99%? Does the achieved fill rate match the target, and does sizing for a fill-rate target instead of a cycle service level change that?
- **E3.** Do the calculators (inverse normal, lead-time demand variability, safety stock for a cycle service level and for a fill rate, expected fill rate, EOQ, pack and minimum-order rounding, reorder point, exponential smoothing, Croston, ABC) agree with references that do not reuse their code, and is the check suite strong enough to catch deliberately broken calculators?
- **E4.** What request rate does the local server sustain for a modest mixed workload on the (busy) machine it was built on?

## 2. Hypotheses

- **H1 (E1).** Smoothing beats a moving average and the naive forecast on smooth, seasonal and trending demand; seasonal-naive wins only where the season is strong and the horizon is long enough that smoothing's lag matters; Croston-type methods win on intermittent and lumpy demand and the corrected version is less biased than classic Croston; with 60 seeds, differences of a few per cent between the best two methods will be detectable but small.
- **H2 (E2).** (s,Q) and (s,S) are nearly identical when demand arrives in small units; periodic review holds less cycle stock but, with a per-order cost, spends more on ordering; sizing for a cycle service level overshoots a fill-rate target because the fill rate counts units, not cycles; the delivered service level falls short of the target where demand is not stationary, because the safety stock is built from one-step errors on a history that lags the change.
- **H3 (E3).** The product passes every check; every broken variant is caught; the normal approximation is close but not exact (expected: a small shortfall under a random lead time, conservatism at low volume).
- **H4 (E4).** The server handles a modest mixed workload at tens of requests per second on a loaded laptop-class machine without errors; throughput does not rise with client threads, because every request runs under one global lock.

## 3. Data and common design

All data is generated. `irp.generator.gen_demand(pattern, n_days, seed, mean)` draws daily demand from `random.Random(seed)`:

| Pattern | Generator |
|---|---|
| smooth | Poisson around a constant mean (6 a day) |
| seasonal | Poisson around a mean with a yearly sine wave (amplitude 50%) and a day-of-week profile |
| trending | Poisson around a mean that grows linearly by 60% a year |
| intermittent | demand on about 20% of days, size 1 plus Poisson(3) |
| lumpy | demand on about 10% of days, size 1 plus a rounded log-normal(1.5, 1.0) |

Seeds are fixed integers; every arm of an experiment is given the same series for the same seed (paired design). Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds (resampling seeds or paired differences, 2,000 resamples, fixed generator seed); p-values come from a paired sign-flip permutation test with Holm correction within each comparison family. Machine metadata (Python, platform, logical CPUs, system CPU load before and during timing runs) is recorded in `results/*_meta.json`.

## 4. Experiment E1: forecast accuracy

**Series.** For each pattern and seed, 169 weeks (1,183 days) of demand summed to weekly periods. **Methods** from `irp.forecast`: naive, moving average (n=4), simple exponential smoothing (smoothing constant chosen on a grid of 0.05 to 1.00 on the training window), seasonal-naive (season 52), Croston and Croston-SBA (smoothing constant 0.1 for both: the product's default, not tuned per series). **Evaluation.** Rolling origin: the first 104 weeks are the smallest training window; the forecast is re-fitted at every origin on data before it; origins advance by the horizon; horizons of 1 and 4 weeks (65 and 16 origins). **Metrics** per (pattern, seed, method, horizon): mean absolute error (MAE, primary because every seed within a pattern has the same scale), bias as a share of mean demand (forecast minus actual), WAPE, and a scaled error (MASE, scaled by the in-sample naive one-step error). **Decision rule** (fixed before the run): the lowest mean MAE is "best"; another method is "not distinguishable" when its Holm-adjusted sign-flip p-value against the best is at least 0.05.

## 5. Experiment E2: reorder policies in the simulator

**Simulator.** `irp.simulator.simulate`: one item, daily steps, a heap of order arrivals. Every 28 days it re-estimates demand from the *sales it has seen* with `planner.estimate_demand` (method rule `auto`, weekly buckets, last 52 weeks) and sets parameters with `policy.plan_policy`; orders come from `policy.order_qty_sq`, `order_qty_ss` and `order_qty_periodic`. Order of events each day: demand, arrivals at the end of the day, review; so an order placed at the end of day t with lead time L is exposed to demand on days t+1 to t+L. Lead times are `max(1, round(Normal(14, 4)))` days drawn from a separate seeded stream; unmet demand is lost. **Item:** cost 10.00, price 16.00, pack 1, MOQ 1, order cost 20.00, holding 25% a year. **Arms:** (s,Q), (s,S) with S = s + Q, and periodic review every 7 days, each sized for cycle service levels 0.90, 0.95, 0.99; plus (s,Q) sized for fill-rate targets 0.90, 0.95, 0.99. **Demand:** the five patterns, 30 seeds each, 364 warm-up days, 56 burn-in days and 365 measured days. **Metrics:** fill rate, cycle service level (share of replenishment cycles without any unmet demand), stockout events, average inventory, orders, total cost (holding + ordering + shortage at price minus cost). **Comparisons:** each arm's fill rate against its target (CI entirely above, consistent, or entirely below); paired cost differences against (s,Q).

## 6. Experiment E3: calculators against independent references

**References that do not reuse the code under test.** (a) A brute-force Monte Carlo of the lead-time-demand model the formulas assume (lead time Normal(Lm, Ls), demand given the lead time Normal(mu L, sigma sqrt(L))), 24 cases of 200,000 draws: the empirical standard deviation checks the variance formula, the empirical P(D <= s) checks the service level of a reorder point, and 1 - E[(D - s)+]/Q checks the fill rate and the fill-rate sizing; half the cases have a fixed lead time (where the formula is exact and the tolerance is four standard errors) and half a random one (where the normal model is approximate and the tolerance is an absolute 0.01, see the change log). (b) Exact Poisson enumeration for demand with a fixed lead time, in the regime lambda x L >= 50 where the approximation is claimed adequate (limit 0.03), and as an informational table elsewhere. (c) Brute-force search of integers for the EOQ and of every feasible pack multiple for the cheapest order quantity. (d) Independent re-implementations of exponential smoothing (closed-form weights), Croston (from the definition) and ABC (sorted cumulative shares). (e) Published constants for the inverse normal. **Mutants.** The same suite is run against 17 subjects, each with one deliberately broken callable (list in section 7). A mutant is *caught* if at least one check fails that passes on the product.

## 8. Interpretation

- **E1.** On weekly buckets the strongest simple method is smoothing with a fitted constant, except for strongly seasonal demand over a four-week horizon, where last year's value beats it and for intermittent or lumpy demand, where Croston with the correction wins narrowly. The gap between smoothing and the corrected Croston on intermittent demand is about 1% of MAE: detectable with 60 paired seeds, not important in practice, and the product's `auto` rule (Croston-SBA for intermittent and lumpy, smoothing otherwise) is consistent with the results. Two cautions: MAE rewards under-forecasting of intermittent series (the WAPE of every method there is 69% or worse), and Croston was run with a fixed constant of 0.1 while smoothing's constant was fitted, which flatters smoothing on the seasonal pattern.
- **E2.** The main finding is about the target, not the policy. A "95% service level" sized as a cycle service level does not deliver a 95% fill rate: on smooth demand it delivered 99.5%, i.e. the planner carries more safety stock than a fill-rate objective needs, and on the seasonal, trending and lumpy patterns the delivered cycle service level was well below the target because the estimate of demand variability comes from one-step errors on a history that lags the change. The fill-rate sizing landed closer to the named figure in only 8 of 15 cells and fell short in 10 of 15. These are properties of the formulas and the estimator under this simulator, and a user who needs the figure to hold should look at the delivered numbers on their own items. (s,Q) and (s,S) differ only when a demand lot is large enough to pull the position well below s, so lumpy demand is the only place a difference was found. Periodic review's cost is dominated by 53 orders a year at 20.00 each; at a much lower order cost the comparison could reverse, which was not tested.
- **E3.** The checks show that the formulas are implemented as stated and that mistakes of the kinds listed would be caught. They do not show that the assumptions hold for real demand: E2 shows they do not for seasonal, trending and lumpy items.
- **E4.** The machine was saturated by other work for every run (system CPU load 68% to 100% before runs and 88% to 100% during them), so these figures describe the server on a busy machine and are well below what an idle one would show; run-to-run spread is large (at eight client threads the interval runs from 6.0 to 20.1 requests/s). H4 expected tens of requests a second and no rise with client threads: the medians (11 to 17) meet that only at the low end, and the intervals overlap, so "flat from 1 to 8 clients" is neither demonstrated nor contradicted. At one client most requests took 13 to 18 ms at the median; the slow tail has two visible causes: the reports page is computed on every request (median 542 ms) and, because the planner's cache is dropped by every write, the first planning page after one of the 5% write requests recomputes every item's plan (the p95 of the dashboard, item list and suggestions pages is 400 to 600 ms; that this is the cause was inferred from the code and not measured separately). Every request runs under one lock, so more clients mostly add queueing, which the p95 growth with client threads shows.

## 9. Limits and threats to validity

- **Synthetic data.** Real demand has promotions, stockouts, substitutions, price changes and calendar effects that none of the generators contains. The generators are one author's choices; the patterns' parameters (6 a day, 20% occurrence, log-normal sizes) set the scale of every result.
- **One item configuration in E2.** One cost structure, one lead-time distribution (mean 14, sd 4), pack size 1, MOQ 1, one review period. Results for other settings were not measured.
- **Simulator conventions.** Lost sales, integer lead times, orders placed at the end of the day, the planner re-estimating every 28 days from observed sales. Different conventions would change absolute numbers.
- **E3's Monte Carlo reference shares the model's assumptions** (independent normal demand, lead time independent of demand); it tests the algebra and the code, not the assumptions. Its tolerance for a random lead time (0.01) was set after seeing a small run, and a bug that moves the service level by less than that under a random lead time is caught only by the fixed-lead-time check.
- **E1 and E2 use 60 and 30 seeds.** Intervals describe the generator's randomness, not uncertainty about any real business. Many comparisons were made; the Holm correction covers the E1 comparisons within a cell, not across cells.
- **E4** was measured with the load generator and server on one busy machine; the numbers are not a capacity claim.
- **Not tested:** other Python versions or operating systems, browsers, assistive technology, security hardening beyond automated request tests, real data, real users.

## 10. Reproduction

```bash
uv run python -m unittest discover -s tests -t .      # all tests
```

## 11. Change log

- **v0.1** Protocol written (questions, hypotheses, methods, statistical plan) before the full runs.
- **v0.2 (2026-11-24)** Results recorded. Deviations and fixes made during the work, in the order they happened:
  - *Product (found by the tests while building M3):* the CSV importer validated money into cents and then passed the cents to code that expects dollars, so every imported price was 100 times too large; it now validates and passes the text. The items export had an extra `active` column that the importer rejected, so exports could not be re-imported; the column was removed. The command line left the database open when `verify` exited non-zero, which blocked deleting the file on Windows; connections are now closed in a `finally`.
  - *Product (found while writing the experiments):* the accuracy report skipped seasonal-naive for items with exactly 52 weeks of history because its training window (half the history) was shorter than a season; it now starts that method's windows after one season (and still skips items without more than 52 weeks).
  - *E3:* the first small run had a check bug (the cost-rounding check was given a request far from the optimum and so reported that the cheapest of two neighbours was not the cheapest overall; it now rounds the true optimum). It also showed that with a random lead time the achieved service level is 0.002 to 0.007 below target, because demand over a random lead time is right-skewed and the normal model ignores that: the single service-level check was split into a strict fixed-lead-time check (four standard errors, formula exact) and a random-lead-time check with an absolute allowance of 0.01 chosen after seeing that run. Mutants are counted as caught only by *new* failures relative to the product's own results (the product had none in the final run). The `meta.json` text describing tolerances was corrected and E3 re-run.
  - *E2:* the summary was extended after the first look at the data: orders per year were added (they explain the periodic-review cost), the lowest-cost table was given paired differences so that nominal wins within noise are not read as wins, and a table for the delivered cycle service level and a count of cells where fill-rate sizing came closer were added. No simulation was changed after the first full run.
  - *E1:* no change after the first full run. Its design (horizons, 104-week window, constants) was fixed before it.
  - *E4:* the load generator starts the real interpreter (`sys._base_executable`) because a virtual environment's `python.exe` on Windows is a launcher that spawns a child; stopping the launcher left a child holding the database file.
